AI as Emerging Institutional Power in Journalism

2026-08-16 · #AI #Journalism #News #Institutional Power

Artificial intelligence is increasingly discussed in journalism as a new tool: something journalists use to write, summarize, translate, analyze data, personalize content, or automate routine work. Much of the debate therefore revolves around questions of use and effects. How effectively can journalists work with AI? How do audiences respond to AI-generated news? What policies should news organizations adopt?

These are important questions, but they may begin too far downstream. A more fundamental question is what happens when journalism becomes dependent on technological systems whose infrastructures, rules, values, and conditions of access are largely determined outside journalism itself.

This essay approaches AI not merely as a tool inserted into existing journalistic routines, but as an emerging configuration of institutional power. Here, “AI” is not a single model. It is a sociotechnical configuration made up of models, training data, technology companies, computational infrastructures, human labor, rules of access, and organizational dependencies. Together, these elements can reshape professional judgment, redistribute authority, create new dependencies, and alter the conditions under which journalism operates.

Core argument. The key question is not only what journalism can do with AI, but what happens when journalism increasingly operates under conditions established by AI systems and actors that it cannot fully inspect, control, or govern.

AI as an emerging configuration of institutional power

Generative AI differs from many earlier newsroom technologies because it intervenes directly in activities journalism has historically connected to human professional judgment: authorship, interpretation, editorial decision-making, originality, and the production of communicative content. AI can generate content, collaborate with journalists and editors, and perform tasks with varying degrees of autonomy within the news production cycle (Guzman & Lewis, 2020; Dodds, Zamith, & Lewis, 2026).

News organizations have responded by developing governance policies emphasizing principles such as transparency and human oversight. Across organizations, these policies often converge around similar concerns, suggesting how rapidly AI has become embedded within journalistic practice (Becker, Simon, & Crum, 2025). At the same time, AI adoption can produce new forms of infrastructural dependency: newsrooms may rely on models, software, cloud systems, and data infrastructures controlled by corporations whose institutional logics are not journalism's own (Hermida & Simon, 2025; Simon, 2022).

The relevant object, then, is larger than “the model.” AI journalism increasingly sits within a configuration involving model developers, cloud companies, chip manufacturers, data providers, annotators, newsroom managers, journalists, audiences, and technical infrastructures. The power of AI lies partly in the relationships among these actors and in journalism's position within them.

This is why dependency is central. Institutional power does not require direct coercion. It can emerge when one actor increasingly relies on systems whose terms of access, operation, or change are determined elsewhere. Journalism may choose to adopt a particular AI system, but once important routines depend on proprietary infrastructures, the practical ability to inspect, modify, reproduce, or withdraw from those systems becomes uneven.

Six forms of AI power

1. Model Architecture Power

Model architecture power refers to the capacity of technical design decisions—such as training-data composition, model architecture, fine-tuning, alignment, and evaluation procedures—to shape how AI systems operate.

These choices influence more than technical performance. They help determine which associations, perspectives, normative assumptions, and forms of judgment a model is more likely to reproduce. Generative systems can generate inaccurate information, reproduce bias, or display implicit orientations toward concepts such as journalistic objectivity (Breazu & Katsos, 2024). Yet the mechanisms producing a particular output are often opaque to the journalist using the system.

This creates a new condition for professional judgment. Journalists must evaluate not only sources and human collaborators, but also outputs generated by systems whose training histories and decision processes they cannot fully inspect. The apparent fluency and neutrality of an AI response can therefore conceal upstream design choices that enter journalistic work without being visible as editorial decisions.

AI also pressures journalism to reconsider the basis of professional authority. As generative systems perform tasks once closely associated with human expertise, journalists increasingly distinguish their professional value through judgment, contextual knowledge, verification, empathy, and ethical reasoning. Research on journalists' “value-motivated use” of AI suggests that professional norms already shape when and how journalists decide to use these systems (Wu, 2024).

2. Customization Power

Customization power concerns the ability to determine the logic through which publics are assembled and addressed.

Journalism has historically organized public attention through editorial judgment. Editors and journalists decide which topics are socially significant, which stories deserve prominence, and which information should form part of a shared informational environment. These decisions are imperfect and contested, but they reflect an editorial logic oriented toward constructing a public agenda.

AI-enabled personalization introduces another logic. Recommendation and personalization systems can rank content according to behavioral histories, inferred interests, predicted preferences, and expected engagement. Micro-targeting can segment audiences still further, allowing different informational packages to reach increasingly granular groups.

The operative question can therefore shift from “What should the public know?” toward “What is this particular user most likely to engage with?”

That distinction matters because personalization does more than distribute already-produced news. It participates in deciding which publics journalism imagines and which informational worlds those publics encounter. Public attention can become organized less around shared editorial constructions of public significance and more around algorithmic predictions of individual relevance and engagement.

3. Corporate Power

Corporate power refers to the structural advantage held by actors that control access to AI systems and determine the conditions under which those systems can be used.

In AI journalism, this power is concentrated among technology corporations that control frontier models and their surrounding ecosystems. These firms can determine API access, pricing, deployment conditions, moderation systems, documentation, model availability, and product integrations. News organizations often depend on these decisions without having reciprocal influence over them.

An especially important dimension is temporal governance. AI companies determine when models change. Updates can alter model behavior. Moderation thresholds can shift. APIs can be deprecated. Model versions can disappear. Capabilities can be added or removed, sometimes without enough transparency for downstream users to fully anticipate the consequences.

For journalism, this creates an unusual institutional condition: a technology on which editorial or analytical work may depend is temporally unstable in ways the newsroom itself does not govern. Corporate power therefore operates through both access governance and temporal governance—determining not only whether journalism can use an AI system, but under what conditions of continuity and change that use remains possible.

4. Data Appropriation Power

Data appropriation power concerns who can extract, reuse, and derive value from informational resources produced by others.

Journalism generates large quantities of professionally created, edited, verified, and contextualized information. That material is also highly valuable for training and improving generative AI systems. This creates a structural tension: news organizations produce knowledge from which AI systems can derive value, while those same news organizations may later become customers of AI infrastructures built partly from knowledge produced by journalism and other cultural industries.

Copyright and licensing disputes make this tension visible, but the institutional issue is broader than any single legal case. It concerns the distribution of value and control. Who produces informational resources? Who determines whether those resources can be reused? Who benefits economically or technologically from their incorporation into AI systems? How much capacity do the original producers retain to govern that process?

Seen from this perspective, the usual direction of adoption can be reversed: rather than journalism simply adopting AI, AI increasingly consumes journalism.

5. Supply-Chain Power

Public discussion of AI often begins at the level of the model. Models, however, depend on extensive material infrastructures.

Supply-chain power refers to the capacity of upstream infrastructure providers to shape the material conditions under which AI can exist and operate. At least three layers are especially important.

First, semiconductor production affects the availability and geopolitical distribution of advanced computational capacity. Second, cloud infrastructure shapes the pricing, scalability, storage, and deployment conditions of large models. Third, energy and environmental resources provide the physical substrate required for large-scale computation.

Journalism therefore occupies a downstream position within a much larger technological chain. Newsrooms may depend on AI systems; AI companies depend on computational infrastructure; and computational infrastructure depends on material supply chains and energy systems.

This creates compounded opacity. The infrastructures shaping AI-assisted journalism can be increasingly distant from the settings in which journalism traditionally exercises oversight. As Crawford (2021) argues, AI's apparently clean and immaterial interface can obscure the physical, geopolitical, and environmental conditions that make computation possible. For news organizations, this means that some of the most consequential conditions of the technologies they adopt are located upstream and outside their direct visibility.

6. Labor Power

AI is also sustained by human labor that frequently disappears from narratives of machine intelligence.

Labor power refers here to the capacity to extract value from human cognitive, annotative, moderation, classification, and evaluation work while rendering that work less visible within representations of AI as autonomous technology.

AI development depends on people who label datasets, evaluate outputs, filter harmful material, classify examples, and provide human feedback. Much of this work has been described as “ghost work” because the human labor underlying automated systems is often obscured (Gray & Suri, 2019).

This invisibility matters in two ways. First, hiding human labor strengthens the appearance of machine autonomy. Second, organizations adopting AI may reproduce that invisibility within their own practices.

For journalism, this creates a normative contradiction. Journalism often defines itself as an accountability institution committed to transparency and attentive to unequal systems of power. Yet AI adoption may embed news organizations within labor arrangements whose conditions are difficult for journalists themselves to see. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether AI makes journalistic work more efficient, but whose work makes that efficiency possible.

How the six powers connect

The six forms of power are analytically distinguishable, but they are not six separate problems. They form a dependency structure.

Journalists, news content + publicsprofessional judgment · knowledge production · public attention

Consider a newsroom introducing an AI-assisted editorial workflow. At first glance, this may appear to be a local organizational decision about productivity. Yet the journalist encounters model architecture power through the outputs and assumptions of the model. News routines can shift through customization power. The organization becomes subject to corporate power through access conditions and product governance. Its content may simultaneously be implicated in data appropriation power. The service relies on computational resources governed through supply-chain power, while the model itself depends partly on forms of human work captured by labor power.

What appears locally as the adoption of a tool is therefore embedded in a much larger chain of institutional relationships.

Global inequality and epistemic dependency

The distribution of these powers is geographically unequal.

Contemporary AI systems are disproportionately developed within a relatively small number of technologically powerful countries and corporations. Their training corpora are heavily shaped by English-language and Western-centered materials, while performance can be weaker in low-resource languages and contexts (Simon & Isaza-Ibarra, 2023).

For news organizations operating outside dominant AI-producing environments, this creates an additional layer of dependency. Developing locally grounded alternatives requires representative training data, computational capacity, technical expertise, financial resources, and infrastructure. These resources are distributed highly unevenly. Newsrooms with fewer upstream resources may therefore be especially likely to depend on off-the-shelf systems designed elsewhere.

The consequence is not only technological dependency. It can become epistemic dependency.

Journalism is rooted in particular linguistic, cultural, political, and professional contexts. When externally developed AI systems become part of routine news production, the assumptions embedded within those systems can gradually enter the infrastructure through which local journalistic decisions are made. Categories, linguistic conventions, evaluative standards, and default assumptions produced elsewhere may begin to function as invisible standards for local practice.

This form of homogenization does not require deliberate cultural imposition. It can arise through structural dependency: when an external infrastructure becomes difficult to avoid, its defaults can become increasingly ordinary and therefore less visible.

The global question is therefore not simply whether newsrooms have equal access to AI. It is whether they have equal capacity to shape the AI systems on which they increasingly depend.

Conclusion

The significance of AI for journalism extends beyond the appearance of generative tools inside newsrooms. AI introduces new actors into journalism's environment, redistributes authority, creates new dependencies, and embeds journalistic work within infrastructures whose operation is often opaque to those who rely on them.

The six forms of power outlined here—model architecture, customization, corporate control, data appropriation, supply chains, and labor—provide one conceptual map for seeing these transformations together. Their value lies not in treating them as a final or exhaustive classification, but in shifting attention from isolated AI applications toward the wider relationships that make those applications possible.

The deepest question is therefore not simply what AI can do for journalism, or what AI might do instead of journalists. It is this:

What happens to journalism when the infrastructures through which it produces knowledge increasingly belong to systems that journalism itself cannot fully inspect, control, or govern?
